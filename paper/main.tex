\begin{abstract}
We study Problem 6.59 of Georgiev, G\'omez-Serrano, Tao, and Wagner
\cite{georgiev2025mathematical}: for a positive integer $n$, let $C(n)$ denote the
size of the largest subset $S$ of the integer grid $[n]^2=\{0,\dots,n-1\}^2$ such
that no three distinct points $a,b,c\in S$ satisfy $|a-b|=|b-c|$ (using exact
integer squared distances, and including degenerate collinear equally-spaced
triples). We independently reproduce and dual-certify, via two independently
implemented exact integer verifiers, the previously published constructions
establishing $C(64)\ge 112$ and $C(100)\ge 164$ \cite{georgiev2025mathematical,
alphaevolverepo}. Using a large-neighborhood-search procedure with exact
integer-linear-programming regional repair (via \texttt{scipy.optimize.milp}),
seeded from these baselines, we ran 33{,}766 independently-solved exact regional
repairs across both grids (8{,}613 on the $64\times64$ grid, 25{,}153 on the
$100\times100$ grid) and found no legal construction exceeding either baseline
within this computational budget. We report this as a genuine negative result, not
a limitation to be glossed over. We do not prove optimality of any construction
reported here, and priority for any apparently-new result remains subject to
external literature verification. All verifiers, tests, and search code are
provided for independent reproduction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Problem 6.59 of \cite{georgiev2025mathematical} asks for the largest
isosceles-triangle-free subset of an $n \times n$ integer grid, where "isosceles"
includes the degenerate case of three collinear, equally spaced points. The paper
reports that AlphaEvolve, an LLM-guided evolutionary search system, found
constructions of size 112 (for $n=64$) and 164 (for $n=100$), improving on a
previously known 110-point construction for $n=64$ found after "many months" of
prior effort. This paper is primarily a verification-and-search report, not a claim
of new mathematical results: we (1) independently reproduce and dual-certify the
published constructions using two independently implemented exact verifiers, (2)
describe a genuine multi-agent research process (a Proposer, a clean-room
Independent Verifier, and an adversarial Red Team, each a separate AI agent
instance with restricted file-write scope) used to generate and audit this work, and
(3) report a systematic, but ultimately negative, computational search for
improving constructions. The emphasis throughout is auditability: every claim in
this paper is backed by a specific file, hash, or reproducible command in the
accompanying repository.

\section{Problem Formulation}

\begin{definition}
For a positive integer $n$, let $[n]^2 = \{0,1,\dots,n-1\}^2$. A finite set
$S \subseteq [n]^2$ is \emph{isosceles-free} if for every three pairwise distinct
points $a,b,c \in S$,
\[
  d^2(a,b) \neq d^2(b,c), \qquad \text{where } d^2(p,q) = (p_x-q_x)^2 + (p_y-q_y)^2.
\]
Equivalently, for every $b \in S$, the multiset of squared distances from $b$ to
every other point of $S$ has no repeated value. This condition explicitly includes
the degenerate case of three distinct collinear equally spaced points (where $b$ is
the midpoint of the segment joining $a$ and $c$). $C(n)$ is the maximum cardinality
of an isosceles-free subset of $[n]^2$.
\end{definition}

We emphasize the condition is a \emph{per-pivot} uniqueness condition: two disjoint
pairs of points in $S$ may legitimately share a squared distance, as long as no
single point of $S$ has two equal-distance neighbors.

\section{Related Work}

The source problem and prior best known values are due to \cite{georgiev2025mathematical}
and the accompanying official problem repository \cite{alphaevolverepo}. A related
but distinct, strictly weaker problem -- avoiding only isosceles \emph{right}
triangles rather than all isosceles triangles -- was studied by
\cite{karolyi2026salem} with an $\Omega(n^{1.3})$ Salem-Spencer-type construction
over the Gaussian integers; since any isosceles-triangle-free set is automatically
isosceles-right-triangle-free but not conversely, that bound is not directly
comparable to (and does not supersede) constructions for Problem 6.59. We checked
two further candidate related papers (an abstract-level check via automated
literature search) and found neither addresses this exact problem; see the
accompanying repository's \texttt{record\_registry.md} for full detail and explicit
scope caveats on this literature check.

\section{Exact Verification}

We implement two independently coded exact integer verifiers (Section~4 of the
accompanying repository): a pivot-distance-uniqueness method and an independent
brute-force triple enumeration (both in \texttt{src/verification/oracle\_verifier.py}),
and a second, clean-room verifier using a vectorized squared-distance matrix with
per-row sorting (\texttt{src/verification\_independent/independent\_verifier.py}),
built by an independent subagent without access to the first verifier's source. All
legality decisions use exact Python arbitrary-precision or bounded-integer
arithmetic; no floating-point distance comparison is ever used for a legality
verdict. The two verifiers agree on all 42 unit/fuzz tests run across the project
(15 for the primary oracle, including a 300-trial randomized cross-check against an
$O(|S|^3)$ brute-force triple enumeration; 21 for the independent verifier,
including its own 200-trial fuzz cross-check against a separately-written brute
force; plus 3 further tests validating the search-side MILP repair against brute
force, and 3 validating the incremental search cache against the oracle). Both
baseline constructions pass both verifiers, and were additionally spot-checked by
hand by the Red Team subagent (sample pairwise squared distances recomputed
manually from the raw coordinate lists).

\section{Search Methodology}

Our search state is an incremental isosceles-free set data structure with
$O(|S|)$-per-operation add/remove/swap (\texttt{src/search/incremental\_state.py}),
stress-tested against the slow oracle across 500 fully-cross-checked random moves in
the project's own test suite and a further 4500+ operations checked by the Red Team,
with zero divergence found in either. Our main search route is a large neighborhood
search seeded from the published baselines: repeatedly destroy a region (a random
axis-aligned box, a row/column band, or a boundary-frame window) and repair it
EXACTLY by solving a 0-1 integer linear program over the region's currently-empty
cells with \texttt{scipy.optimize.milp} (HiGHS) -- as opposed to the greedy
single-pass repair used in the original AlphaEvolve-evolved search program, which
imports but never calls \texttt{scipy.optimize.milp}. Candidates individually
incompatible with the fixed remainder of the set are pre-filtered before the ILP is
built; the MILP formulation's constraints derive from per-pivot squared-distance
groupings (\texttt{src/search/lns\_exact\_repair.py} docstring gives the full
derivation). Every accepted new-best state is re-verified by the slow oracle before
being reported.

\section{Adversarial Multi-Agent Loop}

This project used genuinely independent Claude Code subagents in three roles: a
Proposer (search-strategy design, structural observations, no self-judging),
a clean-room Independent Verifier (no access to the first verifier's implementation),
and a Red Team (actively adversarial, scope-restricted to an audit directory,
required to construct and test invalid inputs, incremental-state divergence hunts,
serialization round-trips, a from-scratch MILP-encoding correctness check on
synthetic instances, and a project-wide scan for overclaiming language). Each role
wrote only within its assigned file scope; promotion of any candidate to a certified
artifact was performed solely by the main agent's \texttt{certify.py} pipeline,
which always re-reads candidates from disk rather than trusting in-memory state.
The Red Team subagent ran six required adversarial attacks (definition correctness
across all pivot orderings, 4500+ incremental-state operations checked after every
move, malformed-input injection across 12 categories, serialization round-tripping,
a project-wide overclaim scan, and MILP-vs-brute-force validation) and returned an
overall PASS verdict, with one low-severity documentation-accuracy finding (the
search module's docstring overstated intermediate-state oracle-verification
frequency; the actual returned output was unaffected) that was subsequently fixed.
Full findings: \texttt{audits/red\_team\_round1.md}.

\section{Structural Observations}

We record two data-supported (but sample-size-2, unproven) observations on the two
published baselines: near-total invariance under central point symmetry (100.0\% for
$n=100$, 96.4\% for $n=64$), and a large empty central region (points confined to
Chebyshev-ring $\le 11$ of a possible 31 for $n=64$; ring $\le 26$ of a possible 49
for $n=100$). See \texttt{hypotheses.md} in the accompanying repository (hypotheses
H-001, H-002) for full detail, evidence, and falsification strategy.

\section{Independent Certification}

A candidate is promoted to DUAL\_VERIFIED only by \texttt{src/verification/certify.py},
which (1) re-reads the candidate strictly from disk, (2) checks structural validity
(bounds, no duplicates, size-field consistency with the actual point count),
(3) runs the primary oracle verifier, and (4) runs the independent clean-room
verifier; only if both agree the set is legal is the candidate copied into
\texttt{results/certified/}. The certified point sets' sha-256 hashes (computed over

\section{Limitations}

We do not prove that either baseline construction, or any construction found in this
project's search, is optimal. Our literature search for competing or superior
constructions (Section 3, and \texttt{record\_registry.md}) used a bounded set of
queries and two abstract-level paper checks; it is not an exhaustive systematic
review. A negative search result (no improvement found) does not establish that no
larger construction exists -- it establishes only that the specific search routes,
seeds, and time budgets used in this session did not find one. All AI-generated
reasoning in this project, including this manuscript, may contain undetected errors
despite the dual-verification and adversarial-review process; exact computational
verification (Section 4) is the only claim in this paper backed by a
machine-checked, reproducible guarantee.

\section{Conclusion}

We independently reproduced and dual-certified the published 112-point ($n=64$) and
164-point ($n=100$) isosceles-triangle-free grid constructions from Problem 6.59,
and ran a genuine multi-agent research loop including an exact-ILP-based large
neighborhood search (33{,}766 total regional repairs across both grids). No
construction exceeding either published baseline was found within this session's
compute budget, and our literature audit found no evidence of any external
construction doing so either. We do not prove optimality, and priority for any
future apparently-new result remains subject to external verification. See
\texttt{FINAL\_REPORT.md} in the accompanying repository for the full final verdict
and disclosed limitations, including an explicit statement that this was a single
bounded research session rather than a longer unattended search.

\section{Verification Algorithms}
\textbf{Pivot-distance method} (primary oracle): for each point $b \in S$, compute
the squared distance to every other point of $S$; reject if any value repeats.
$O(|S|^2)$ time, exact integer arithmetic throughout.

\textbf{Brute-force triple method} (cross-check): for every ordered triple of
distinct points $(a,b,c)$ with $b$ the candidate apex, reject if
$d^2(a,b) = d^2(b,c)$. $O(|S|^3)$, used only for cross-checking on small $|S|$.

\textbf{Independent verifier}: builds the full pairwise squared-distance matrix via
vectorized integer (int64) arithmetic, then for each row (pivot) sorts the
off-diagonal values and scans for adjacent duplicates; any candidate duplicate is
re-confirmed with Python arbitrary-precision arithmetic before being reported.
